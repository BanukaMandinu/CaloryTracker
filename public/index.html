Profile

How the numbers are calculated

Every figure here is an estimate. Population equations are typically within about ±10% for an individual, so track trends over weeks rather than single days. This is not medical advice. Consult a qualified clinician before a large diet change.

1. BMI

BMI = weight(kg) ÷ height(m)². The WHO bands are under 18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25–29.9 and 30 or more. BMI cannot tell muscle from fat, so a muscular person can read "overweight". Use body fat % alongside it.

2. Body-fat %

  • Measured value (DEXA or a smart scale), if you enter one.
  • U.S. Navy tape method (Hodgdon & Beckett, 1984) from waist, neck and, for women, hip. Typical error is 3–4 points.
    Men: 495 ÷ (1.0324 − 0.19077·log₁₀(waist−neck) + 0.15456·log₁₀(height)) − 450
    Women: 495 ÷ (1.29579 − 0.35004·log₁₀(waist+hip−neck) + 0.22100·log₁₀(height)) − 450
  • Deurenberg equation (1991) if nothing else is available. It is the least accurate option.
    BF% = 1.20·BMI + 0.23·age − 10.8·(1 male / 0 female) − 5.4

3. Resting energy (BMR)

Mifflin-St Jeor (1990) was the most accurate of the common equations in the Frankenfield 2005 review: 10·kg + 6.25·cm − 5·age + 5 (men) / −161 (women). If you enter a measured body-fat %, the app switches to Katch-McArdle, 370 + 21.6 × lean mass (kg), which accounts for muscle.

4. Total daily energy expenditure

Most apps multiply BMR by a vague "activity factor", which is usually wrong. Here the parts are counted separately:

Expenditure = BMR × baseline + steps + workouts

  • Baseline (your activity level: ×1.2 mostly sitting, ×1.3 light, ×1.4 active, ×1.5 very active, excluding workouts) covers resting metabolism, the thermic effect of food (about 10% of intake; Westerterp 2004) and ordinary sedentary living.
  • Steps: step length ≈ 0.415 × height (men) or 0.413 × height (women). Net walking cost is about 0.5 kcal per kg per km (from the ACSM walking equation, 0.1 mL O₂/kg/m). So kcal = 0.5 × kg × steps × step length(km). Baseline living already includes a little walking, so this slightly overcounts.
  • Workouts use MET values from the Compendium of Physical Activities (Ainsworth 2011). kcal = (MET − 1) × kg × hours. Subtracting 1 MET avoids counting resting energy twice. If your watch or machine gives a figure, you can enter that instead, though wearables can be off by 30–90% (Shcherbina 2017).

5. Deficit and surplus

Balance = calories eaten − expenditure. A negative balance is a deficit. The rule of thumb is ≈7,700 kcal per kg of body weight (3,500 kcal/lb; Wishnofsky 1958). It is a decent short-term guide but overestimates long-term loss, because your metabolism adapts as you lose weight (Hall et al., Lancet 2011). Weekly weight changes, not this number, are the ground truth.

6. Goal plan

Required daily deficit = (current − goal weight) × 7700 ÷ days left. The daily calorie budget is expenditure minus that deficit. The app warns if the plan needs more than ~1% of body weight per week, a deficit over 1,000 kcal/day, or intake below your BMR. Those levels risk muscle loss and nutrient shortfalls.

7. Macro and nutrient targets

  • Protein: 1.6 g/kg is the point where extra protein stops adding meaningful muscle in resistance-trained people (Morton et al., 2018). The default is 1.8 g/kg, and 1.6–2.2 is a reasonable range.
  • Fat: 20–35% of calories (default 28%), never below about 0.6 g/kg.
  • Carbs: the remaining calories.
  • Energy per gram (Atwater): protein 4, carbohydrate 4, fat 9 kcal/g.
  • Fiber ≥ 14 g per 1,000 kcal (Institute of Medicine). Sugar and saturated fat under 10% of calories, and sodium under 2,000 mg (WHO guidance).

How daily maintenance (calories out) is estimated

Formulas give a population average, and an individual can differ by 10–20%. So the app works in two stages.

  1. Formula. Resting burn (Mifflin-St Jeor, or Katch-McArdle with a measured body-fat %) × your daily activity level (1.2 mostly sitting to 1.5 very active), plus steps and workouts. To avoid counting walking twice, only steps above what your activity level already assumes (about 3,000 a day for sitting up to 11,000 for very active, from the Tudor-Locke & Bassett step bands) are added, at about 0.5 kcal per kg per km. Past days use the weight you had on that day.
  2. Personal calibration. Energy balance says calories out = average intake − (weight trend × 7,700 kcal/kg) (Hall 2008). Over the last 4 weeks the app measures your average logged intake and the slope of your weigh-ins, and compares the result with the formula for the same days. Every estimate is then nudged by that ratio, limited to ±20% and scaled by how much data there is: at least 10 logged days, 4 weigh-ins spread over 10+ days and food logged on 60% of days. It pauses if your weight swings by more than 1.5 kg/week.

Calibration is only as good as your logging: if you under-report food, your maintenance will look too low. Weigh yourself at a consistent time, and log most days. You can switch it off, or enter your own maintenance figure, in Profile.

Goal-based deficit and your own plan

Your calorie burn is not constant: it falls by roughly 20–25 kcal/day for every kg lost (and rises as you gain), so the same daily deficit loses weight more slowly over time (Hall et al. 2011, 2012). The app uses a simplified dynamic energy-balance model. With Δ = kg to change, T = days to your goal date and ρ = 7,700 kcal/kg:

deficit = 22 × Δ ÷ (1 − e^(−22·T/ρ))

For a typical goal this is roughly 5–20% stricter than the plain rule Δ × 7,700 ÷ T, which ignores that adaptation. The plan is then capped at a safe pace: about 1% of body weight per week (never more than 1,000 kcal/day) when losing, and 500 kcal/day when gaining. If your date is too aggressive, the app shows the date you would actually reach at the safe pace. Your daily target never drops below 1,500 kcal for men or 1,200 kcal for women, the usual minimum for unsupervised dieting (NHLBI 1998). In Profile → Calorie plan you can instead enter your own daily deficit or your own fixed calorie target. Very large deficits should be supervised by a clinician.

Water

The default goal is 35 ml per kg of body weight, rounded to the nearest 100 ml and kept between 1.5 and 4 litres. This is a general guide. EFSA's adequate intake for total water (drinks plus the roughly 20% that comes from food) is about 2.0 L/day for women and 2.5 L/day for men (EFSA 2010). Needs rise with heat, altitude and exercise, and some medical conditions or medicines change them. Set your own goal in Profile, and ask a clinician if you have a condition that affects fluid intake.

8. Scanning without AI fees

Barcodes are decoded in your browser: the built-in BarcodeDetector where available (Chrome/Android), otherwise the open-source ZXing library. The code is looked up in Open Food Facts, a free crowd-sourced database that needs no API key. Coverage varies by country and entries can contain mistakes. Photo identification uses CLIP (Radford et al., 2021), a small open-source image model that runs in your browser through Transformers.js. It compares your photo with the names in the built-in food list (zero-shot), so it only recognises foods on that list, and it can confuse similar-looking foods. A photo cannot measure portion size, so it proposes a typical serving that you should adjust. Use Search or Manual for anything it misses. Packaged-food labels may legally deviate from true content by up to 20% in some jurisdictions.
